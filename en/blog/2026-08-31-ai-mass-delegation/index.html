Mass Delegation: Handing Most of My Work to AI

Around July 2026 I began handing most of my work to AI, and missed verification led me to change the completion condition.

(Written in 2026, looking back.)

After moving procedures into skills, around July 2026 I began delegating work broadly to AI. This was also when I started building in-house AI tools to support slot development.

The problem

I wanted to delegate work beyond coding tools. From June I tested AgentB, a personal agent in Slack, trying skills, session handoff, calls, and local model routing in turn. The biggest limit was the platform: inside Slack it only moved when tagged.

Background and framing

I split the cost of delegation into three: dividing the work, passing context, and checking results. With more sessions, tools can reduce the first two, but checking grows with the session count. Most problems in this period came from that third part.

In a September team conversation I left this line.

AI does the work, people do the verification

In the same place I also wrote that our team was verifying by hand because of engine limits. Work had sped up, but checking was still in human hands.

Attempts

I split work across many sessions, and in August began using automatic review and hooks. Time spent dividing work and checking results grew beyond time spent typing code. I also split models by stage to save usage. The super bonus case I shared with the team in September looked like this.

Stage What was delegated
Server Structure design in plan mode, development and simulation in goal mode
Client Structure design, correction to stay within our existing work style
Flow Sequence-focused and presentation-focused work on different models

Where my judgment changed

Delegating more meant verification after changes was often skipped: the agent reported done without actually running anything. So I changed the completion condition from the agent’s report to actual execution results.

In August I gathered my feedback for a retrospective. Instead of treating all follow-up fixes as agent mistakes, I sorted them into direct misunderstanding, spec change, defect first appearing at runtime, and incomplete evidence. This sorting continues in the post on reorganizing skills.

With AgentB in July I tested a tool for placing phone calls and routing a local model to Codex. The table’s extra correction step against our existing work style, after client structure design, turned my March coding style feedback into procedure.

I used the same approach in in-house game jams. In the February-March 2026 AI jam I built a minigame hub, core systems, and agent work rules, and in the July-September new game exploration I built several candidates, compared them, and focused on one.

Standards for dividing work also formed gradually in this period. Work that didn’t touch the same files ran in parallel, and resources usable by one at a time, like Unity, got an order. Design and implementation were split so structure was set first and style matched later.

Human time moved from writing to reading. But I have no record measuring how much it moved, only a similar impression.

Human checks didn’t disappear after I changed the completion condition. What changed is what I read first. Before, I read the agent’s summary and decided whether to trust it. Now I look first at commands run and their scope, and ask about any missing layer first. It’s less that reading shrank than that my questions became concrete. Game jams were similar. Even when technical checks passed, human and market acceptance stayed marked as unconfirmed. Marking scope this way can slow finishing, but I judged it cheaper than saying something is done by mistake and coming back. That isn’t measured; it’s my interpretation. Running many sessions doesn’t fit everything either. Work that sets structure went better as one long conversation, and the rise in average session turns in October may partly reflect more such work.

Reflection

It’s true that session counts quadrupled, but that doesn’t mean four times the work. Given the share of one-turn sessions, many of them may have been short runs. I use this number only to show scale.

The 90% figure in August shouldn’t be taken at face value either. It’s a regex count of commands, and it misses tests inside scripts and screen checks. Still, it’s hard to deny the direction that many of my edits went unchecked.

This doesn’t seem unique to me. Anthropic’s post on long-running agents says Claude tended to mark features complete after only unit tests or curl checks, and that having it test like a person with browser automation helped. But that’s an observation from a specific environment, and I had to check separately whether it carries over to Unity work such as slots.

Delegating a lot is easy to describe as ability, but in my case the amount I hadn’t checked grew with it. That’s my interpretation.

My experience with the personal agent AgentB was similar. Calls and local model routing could be tried, but Slack’s way of invoking agents couldn’t be fixed inside the tool. Around then I first felt that adding tools doesn’t increase delegation if the starting point of work doesn’t change. This is also my interpretation, and the thought led to the GS App design.

The numbers here come from my own usage records and don’t transfer directly to other teams or tools. When deciding whether to delegate more, I think it’s right to measure checking costs in your own environment first.

General lessons

The more I delegated, the more my work shifted from typing code to dividing and checking. And for the team to use AI tools, there had to be an entry point outside the terminal.

Before delegating more, I look at where checking costs grow. If the completion condition is execution results, what needs looking at shrinks even with many sessions.

Next steps

That entry point became GS App. How to trust execution results led to Runtime Lab and the benchmark.

Even when part of checking moves to machines, some things remain for people. How far it can move is the remaining question.
