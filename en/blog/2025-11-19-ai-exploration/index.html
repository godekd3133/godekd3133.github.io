Exploration: The First Time I Used Codex and Claude Code

The period when I first used Codex in August 2025 and Claude Code in November, and kept testing them through February 2026.

(Written in 2026, looking back.)

I first used Codex in August 2025 and Claude Code in November. Before that the team was already using Claude models through GitHub Copilot Chat, and I was the one asking to enable new models and sharing them with the team. This post records testing tools during the period when I was attending both work and school.

The problem

What I wanted to know was whether this worked on real slot code. In July 2025 I had Copilot Chat with Claude 4 Sonnet build the server interface for a new game and wrote up the results in the team channel. I fed in the design and math documents as csv, and had it delete unneeded interfaces and rewrite from scratch. My note was that with clear prompts and code-indexer MCP tools, this was an area that could be improved enough.

Background and framing

When I look at this period, I split the question into three. Can the tool write code? How well does it keep the project’s context? How do I limit the damage when it goes wrong? At the time I looked only at the first. The other two became questions only after I got stuck.

Codex stayed at 12 sessions a month or fewer in this period. Of 147 Claude prompts in January 2026, 131 were slash commands, though the recording method may have affected that value.

A daily update from January 2026 also records a change in how I worked. Handing over everything and only managing took a lot of teaching time and pushed the schedule back. Once I planned all the work first and used prompts for implementation only, speed started to come.

Part of the instructions I shared with the team in January 2026 also remains. It splits unclear situations after context summarization into four, and sets what to ask in each.

In December 2025 I shared with the team that adding a line to the instruction file, saying to update it when the structure grows or changes, made work fit the structure better. My interest shifted from writing good prompts toward leaving context in the project.

Where my judgment changed

Two things got in the way. One was safety. In January 2026 I saw a case elsewhere of a home directory being wiped and warned the team to manage permissions carefully. The other was losing context after summarization and proceeding on guesses. So I added a rule to the instructions: never guess, ask.

A person had to steer the work back, but I noted that while user-driven, it made the work clearer.

In July 2025 I also shared another developer’s workflow with the team, one that split work small and limited what AI could touch. I was looking at the difference between delegating all at once and in small pieces.

Looking back at those attempts in terms of cost, there were three ways of working. Handing over everything at once keeps instructions short but makes fixing long. Planning everything and delegating only implementation makes instructions longer but keeps results closer to my thinking. Splitting the scope small takes human time to divide. The January 2026 update records my move to the second way. In that way, AI doesn’t own the design, so it’s hard to use in areas whose structure I don’t know.

Rereading my writing from this period shows where my interest moved. In July 2025 I evaluated the response structure of the output. In December I talked about sentences in an instruction file. In January 2026 I talked about permissions and compaction. Interest moved from results to conditions. But the models were also improving quickly in that time. These records can’t separate whether results improved because my method did, or whether better models made it feel that way.

Reflection

What I can state as fact is that I added instruction files, permissions, and a re-check rule in turn. I didn’t measure how much results improved because of them. My February sentence that it had “developed more than we thought” is also an impression.

There is a reason to be careful with impressions. In METR’s 2025 randomized controlled trial, 16 experienced open-source developers took 19% longer when using AI, yet believed they had sped up. My environment and tools differ, so it doesn’t transfer directly, but the point against relying on impressions still holds.

So it’s my interpretation that the trial and error here became material for later rules. Some people likely used the tools well without such rules in the same period, and nothing shows my method was the only path.

The compaction rule has limits too. An instruction works only if the agent reads it, and the agent asks only if it notices that context was lost. I didn’t count how much guessing this rule reduced. I wrote that work became clearer, but that is one person’s observation.

General lessons

I used the tools in bursts and stopped, not steadily, so I see this as before they became a way of working. But the ingredients that later became skills came from here: leave context in files, limit permissions, and make it ask when unsure.

In February 2026, including Unity integration, I solved a presentation timing issue that involved FSMs, Behaviours, and symbol assets together, and wrote in the team channel that it seemed more advanced than we had thought. After that I began putting it into actual work.

When I enter another tool, I want to check three things first: where context is kept, how far permissions go, and whether there’s a way to make it stop when it doesn’t know. These questions still work when the tool changes.

Next steps

If the question then was whether it was useful, the next was how much I could delegate on a product I wanted to use. KBO Fans was one such attempt, and the work side continues in Into Daily Work.

What I regret looking back is not leaving a baseline for comparison. Had I measured how long the same task took a person and a tool, today’s judgment would be easier. I later tried to fill this gap with the benchmark.
