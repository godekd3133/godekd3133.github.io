What I Learned Building Reactor Rush

While implementing a mechanic where you catch enemy bullets and fire them back, I wrote down what automated checks can confirm and what only a person playing can tell.

Reactor Rush is a single-player 3D action expedition built in Three.js. You pass three zones across sky islands and a storm power plant and defeat a boss. After dropping the duel two days earlier, it still needed a signature control. The product doc’s first signature was stealing bullets with a perfect dodge, and the directly controlled extension of that is magnetic capture. Q or the capture button gathers incoming bullets, and pressing again fires them back.

The question

Adding a control is easy. Getting players to bother using it is hard. And the faster you build with AI and automated checks, the easier it is to mix “it runs” with “it is fun.” This post looks at both problems through the magnetic capture.

Background and framework

I split a control into three stages: input (does a press survive?), rules (what changes when you use it?), and judgment (do you weigh when to use it?). The first two can be confirmed by code and checks, and the last needs people playing. My sources are the source code and the repository’s verification scope document.

The fix for lost quick taps came down to this class.

/** A quick press must survive until the game can actually accept a volley. */
export class RogueFireIntent {
  private pending=false;
  request(){this.pending=true;}
  shouldFire(held:boolean){return held||this.pending;}
  accepted(){this.pending=false;}
  cancel(){this.pending=false;}
}

What I tried

  • Holding the field open while moving gathers up to 12 bullets. Pressing again counterattacks, and it fires automatically after 1.5 seconds.
  • The Return Coil evolution cuts cooldown by 3 seconds only when countering with 3 or more bullets. Empty captures and 1 to 2 bullets get no bonus.
  • Stormeye’s shield takes only 20% from regular fire but full damage from bullets stolen by capture or dodge counter.

Tying the boss shield and evolution rules to capture put a reason to use it inside combat.

Recording checks and fun separately

Having learned from the duel that passing checks says nothing about fun, I keep a table of what was checked and how far. The roughly 33-second clear by an automated controller isn’t used as evidence of human clear time or fun. A new-player expedition finished by real browser input alone is recorded separately.

What I learned

Adding a control and creating a reason to use it were different jobs. And the faster automated play records came, the more they had to be written separately so as not to be confused with human experience. This distinction connects to not counting AI persona interviews as real user validation in Career Center.

A table separating levels of evidence

The repository’s verification scope document records what evidence confirmed each item. Here is part of it.

Item Evidence Level

The new player run was also recorded as a procedure. From zero wins, using only real input, I started on the sky island, chose evolutions twice, and finished in 39.6 seconds. The result was 27 kills, 5 perfect dodges, 11 captured shots, and 78 damage taken. I also checked that saves held after reconnecting. Still, this is input from the person who built it, and it can’t stand in for what a first-time player does.

Writing small-screen problems as numbers made them easy to recheck. When the crown skin overlapped the mission panel, I split showroom camera distance into default 14.5, crown 16.8, and storm 15.4, then checked at 360×740 and 320×568. After that change I did not rerun all 60 regression checks, and the document says so. I think how far you ran things needs writing down to be usable for the next decision.

Shield rules also have room to adjust. The fortified turret, with 120 health and a 60 shield, went into the middle zone, while the first zone and boss rematch stayed unchanged. That was a decision about scope for a new enemy. Its effect on difficulty needs human evaluation.

Input fixes also came from the order people use things. With no input a dodge moves away from enemies, and when a wall blocks it, it switches to the side. On touch, auto-fire is on by default so the first control gets full attention. Checks can confirm these defaults work, but not whether beginners use them well. So the unconfirmed column in the document doesn’t shrink. To fill it, I decided I need other people’s play records rather than more automated checks.

In the next evaluation, I plan to look first at when a player first presses capture and what they do facing the shield.

Reflection

The verification scope document still lists graphics evaluation, fun and return visits, physical devices, and audio as unconfirmed. There is also a desktop frame sample from a recent build, but it is a short measurement in one environment, so I don’t use it as evidence of phone performance.

Saying the boss shield and evolution rules create a reason to use capture is my interpretation. The code confirms the rules work that way, but I don’t know whether players feel it. The rule that cuts regular fire to 20% damage could even feel like a penalty forcing capture. Discussions of game feel also find it hard to judge how a control feels from numbers alone.

A clear by automated play is evidence that the game runs to the end. I don't use it as evidence of difficulty or fun.

General lessons

  • Split a control into input, rules, and judgment, and write down how far each was confirmed.
  • In input handling, hold the intent of a press until the moment it can be processed.
  • Next to an automated measurement, write what it does not prove.

What’s next

This game still needs human play evaluation. On October 9 I reviewed the current build by playing it myself, and next comes others’ evaluation.
