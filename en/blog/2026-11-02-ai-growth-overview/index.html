How Has My Way of Working with AI Tools Changed?

Based on my use of Codex and Claude Code, I divided the period from August 2025 to now into five stages.

The problem

I put this together looking back at my Codex and Claude Code usage records and what I wrote in team channels at the time. The biggest turning point was starting to delegate work broadly to AI around July 2026. Each stage links to its post.

Background and framing

This post rests on remaining Codex and Claude Code records and what I wrote at the time. Record quality varies by period, so I state the boundaries first.

How my thinking moved

Is it useful?How do I leave context?How does the team start together?How do I know it's done?How do I know it got better?

The first question was whether it worked on real code. My note in summer 2025 said prompts and code indexers could improve it. Then I moved toward leaving context between sessions with CLAUDE.md and context files, the pattern in my February 2026 report.

Feedback I kept repeating became skills, and when the terminal became a barrier I built GS App. Delegating more meant I couldn’t trust reports of being done, so I checked the screen with Runtime Lab. Unable to say whether skills had improved, I built the benchmark, where I had to fix my own scorer first.

One problem and its fix

As delegation grew, verification after changes was often skipped.

Problem

Reading an agent's done report at face value misses what never ran.

Action and change

Completion now means execution results, with how far things ran reported separately. The share of Codex editing sessions with a verification command rose from 32% in August to 82% in September. Repeated automation may have inflated this, so I read it only as a direction.

What I handle

In February 2026 and now, my job is similar: correct AI when it goes on a wrong assumption, set standards, and do the final check. What changed is that those standards now live in skills, tools, and scorers rather than conversations.

I also wrote how my work changed in each of the five stages.

Earlier risks didn’t vanish when a stage ended. I still watch permissions and context. The table lists the problem most visible at the time.

To restate my job in one sentence, it’s setting standards and checking whether they hold. That sentence is also an interpretation of work records. I have no measurement of my time by item. How much implementation AI handles varies by task, and I still look directly at slot presentation judgment and structure decisions. So I don’t generalize that “I don’t write code.” To say I’ve gotten better with tools, I’d need to compare results, and the benchmark’s main experiment hasn’t run. This post records change; it doesn’t prove effect. So I tried to leave out causal sentences. I can say sessions got longer after I used skills, but not that skills made them longer.

Reflection

The uncomfortable fact in the table is that my way of instructing barely changed. Median prompts are short, plan-first requests are low, and rework hasn’t fallen. This data doesn’t support any claim that my prompts became more refined.

What changed sits outside prompts. Instructions, skills, hooks, and verification tools support short instructions. That might mean short instructions now suffice, or that my habit of fixing afterward stayed. This data can’t tell which.

I’m careful with the word growth as well. It’s true that the range of use widened, but nothing measures better result quality. As in METR’s study, impression and reality can differ. So please read these five stages as the order in which my questions changed, not as levels of achievement. The staging itself is my interpretation looking back.

Gaps in the data affect conclusions too. The four months with originals can’t be compared directly with earlier periods, and how calls are recorded also changed by tool. In places I can’t separate whether a value moved because of my behavior or the recording. These tables also shouldn’t be used to compare with other people.

The tools themselves changed a lot as well. Their default behavior shifted over this period, so some values that look like changes in my behavior may be the tools’ doing.

General lessons

When reviewing your own tool use, look at behavior metrics and data boundaries before usage volume. Putting unfavorable metrics in the same table shows better what to fix next.

Next steps

Two things remain unanswered. I put the main experiment on hold, so I can’t say how much skills change results, and I haven’t measured how much faster team work became. Most personal products still need real user validation. I’d like the next stage’s post to answer one of these three.

Specifically, I want to examine the relation between plan-first requests and rework turns per project. I also need to settle how to keep originals so records don’t break when tools change.
