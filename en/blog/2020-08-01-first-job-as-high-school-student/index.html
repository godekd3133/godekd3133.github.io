Starting My First Job as a High School Student

A record of joining 9M Interactive in my final year of high school and working on the core and unit AI of a card-deck merge battle game.

(Written in 2026, looking back.)

In July 2020, as a third-year high school student, I joined 9M Interactive as a junior client programmer on the Sheed team. I graduated from Seoul Digitech High School in March 2021 and stayed at the company until December 3, 2021. The competition and Bright Burn’s release from earlier posts fell in the same year.

The question

If joining a company in high school is written only as the start of a career, what I gained stays hard to explain. So I compare it with the time I worked project by project, and look at how the unit of work changed.

Background and framework

My sources are my cover letter and portfolio from then, and the dates on my resume. I did not use company internal material. I compare four things: duration, what ends the work, shared code, and the scope of my role.

My portfolio from then has a joke “passive skill”: working with several people raises work speed by 50%. It is written like a game character sheet and is not a measured number. Still, it is a record of me saying I liked working with others.

Why I started

My self-introduction gave my reason for joining a company: I wanted to carry on the value of learning and growth, heading toward endless new ground, at work as well. What I wrote about a year later became this post’s conclusion.

What I tried

I developed a card-deck merge battle game.

  • Simulation core for card-deck merge battles
  • Combat unit AI and crowd movement
  • Out-game UI

Unlike the assignments I’d made alone, this was one project carried on as a team for over a year. I kept making things outside work too.

The five Jack’s Bean members were high school friends then working at game companies. Morbido used QuickSheet to link spreadsheets and data, which was my experience of keeping design values outside the code.

Where my judgment shifted

The biggest change in what I wrote after joining was about my career. I wrote that after pouring affection into one project for a year, my doubts about becoming a game developer turned into certainty.

The same piece said that meeting many people in the industry made me more mature as a person and a better programmer.

Before joining

I built assignment by assignment toward competitions and releases. Whether game development suited me as a career was still an open question.

After joining

Carrying one project for a year turned those doubts into certainty.

In Jack’s Bean’s Corona Yellow, I managed resources with the Addressable package and collection data with a Custom Editor and ScriptableObject. I met Addressables again when building CDN patching for Pack & Clash in 2024.

What I learned

Handling unit AI and crowd movement was my first time at a company translating a game’s combat rules into code. From February 2021 I served as an industrial technical personnel, finishing on December 23, 2023.

What outside work filled in

Much of my company work can’t be published, so I also looked at what I practiced outside the company in the same period. This is confirmed by my public portfolio and repositories.

In the right column, the link between Addressables and Pack & Clash is fact. The other cells are links I draw looking back now, so they are closer to my interpretation.

Morbido’s speech bubbles are a small case with a clear structure. Lines aren’t in the code: they load from a spreadsheet as a string array, and a coroutine walks the queue to print them. Whoever edits a line doesn’t need to open the code. I suspect five people working together created the need for this split. That reason isn’t in the records and is my guess.

The combat core and unit AI at work also changed how I worked. In competition games I could set the rules myself, but at work I had to turn given rules into code and maintain them as the project went on. It was a starting point for later turning slot rules into server and client code at my next company.

Set side by side, the dates show that graduation and the start of my service fall close together. I started service in February 2021, graduated the next month, and the service continued at my next company until December 2023. It is fact that I changed companies during service, and the sources for this post don’t explain why, so I don’t state a reason. My work at the first and next companies shares turning game rules into code. The genre changed from crowd battles to slots, but I think the condition of implementing rules without error stayed the same. That link is my interpretation.

Most activities in the table can be rechecked through public repositories or videos, so I used them as comparison material here.

Reflection

The changes I can confirm as fact are the duration and scope of the work. The line about doubt turning into certainty is something I wrote at the time, so it is enough evidence for what I thought then. That I am still making games is also fact. Concluding that my first job caused that is my interpretation.

I also can’t say that starting work early is always better. I entered university only in 2024, so I met documents and the user’s perspective from university late. Seeing that as something I postponed by starting work early is also my interpretation. Internal reviews and the game’s results are not material I can publish, so they are not in this post.

The limit of this post: it relies only on my own writing. The view of people who worked with me is not here.

General lessons

  • Early career changes can be compared if you write them as duration and scope of responsibility rather than job titles.
  • What you wrote at the time shows your judgment then, not that it was correct.
  • Work outside the company is a place to try tools the company doesn’t use. For me that was Addressables and keeping data apart from code.

What came next

In December 2021 I moved to Bagelcode and began writing server logic on top of the Unity client. That continues in Taking On Both Slot Server and Client.
