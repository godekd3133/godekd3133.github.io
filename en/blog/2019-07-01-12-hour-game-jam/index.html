What I Learned from 12-Hour Game Jams

Centered on the split viewport in What Up Buddy, a look at which implementations kept recurring across the games I finished in 12 hours.

(Written in 2026, looking back.)

In my high school self-introduction I wrote that I had grown tired of playing inside fixed frames and rules, and became curious whether a game I made and played myself would be more fun.

That curiosity took me to a high school with a game development major, and I kept entering AppJam, Highthon, the Korea Youth Game Jam, and my school’s jam. Teams of three or four had to finish a game in 12 hours. At the same time I was writing frameworks from scratch in DirectX 9 in the skills club, so game jams were more like my practice ground for finishing things in Unity.

What I tried

My 12-hour projects include What Up Buddy, Santa Overslept!, and a Cookie Run clone for personal study.

What Up Buddy (July 2019) is a quarter-view co-op game. Two people control one game, so the screen had to be split.

One camera per playerPoint each camera's Target Texture at a Left or Right render textureA main camera films a scene with the two textures side by side

The rest stayed simple too. Items were an enum that separated their effects, and CarSpawner picked a random direction and position, then checked that nothing would overlap before spawning.

Santa Overslept! (December 2019) is a side-scrolling casual arcade game. One array of gift boxes handled both spawning and input.

Where my judgment shifted

The decision I made when first needing a feature looked similar from game to game. For split screen I didn’t look for a way to draw it myself and used render textures, an engine feature. In the Cookie Run clone I updated only objects visible to the camera and left maps to the tilemap. Around then the skills club had me parsing obj files by hand, so I was choosing differently what to build and what to delegate depending on the tool.

Side by side, the two games repeat the same choices.

What I learned

With a fixed deadline, you combine a few methods you already know rather than deliberating over a new structure. This was also when I gained experience sharing code with other people. Unlike the solo skills club, a jam meant merging code in the same project.

The same approach with a little more time produced Ttang Pa Seo Don Na Oh Nya?!, built in 20 hours and released in 2020. I split level design into several pieces and joined the ends so it kept scrolling.

What came next

After repeating short jams I moved on to a six-month team project, recorded in Bright Burn. Jams continued afterward: I took first place in the entertainment category at the 20th AppJam in 2022, and kept joining in-house game jams after starting work.
