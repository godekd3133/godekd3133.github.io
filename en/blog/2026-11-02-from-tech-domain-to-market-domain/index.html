Handing Implementation to AI Made Me Look Harder at the Market

Since I began handing implementation to AI, the question that matters more is no longer how to build something, but what to build so it succeeds in the market.

The problem

For a long time I explained my value through technology. In high school I wrote frameworks in DirectX with no engine, and at work I built both slot server logic and Unity clients. I thought growth meant finding better implementations, like adopting UniTask or improving reel systems.

That changed around July 2026, when I began handing most of my work to AI.

Background and framing

I split my work into three questions: can it be built, was it built correctly, and is it worth building? AI tools greatly lowered the cost of the first, and verification tools can now handle part of the second. The third still has no answer without users.

Can it be built

Answered by implementation and AI tools.

Was it built correctly

Answered by tests and screen checks.

Is it worth building

Answered only by actual use and payment.

Evidence

The current validation stage of my personal products looks like this.

I don't yet have market experience worth claiming. This post isn't a success story; it's a note on what I need to learn next.

What changed

What used to be hard

How do I implement this? Which structure is faster and safer?

What is harder now

Is this worth building? Who uses it and why, and will it work in the market?

Technology still matters. You need it to judge and verify results. But as implementation takes less time, the weight of my work has moved elsewhere. When you can build anything quickly, it also becomes easy to build the wrong thing quickly.

From the technical domain to the market domain

Delegate implementation to AIBuilding gets cheaperWhat to build becomes the bottleneckTest market hypotheses first

So the expertise I need to grow has changed too. Alongside choosing technology, it now matters as much to understand which products succeed in the market and to develop them efficiently.

Recent work shows the shift.

Work What I thought about first
Internal new game exploration Building several candidates, comparing the evidence, then focusing on one
Slot development workflow How the team can try new slots faster to the same standard
Personal products Whose problem it solves and how to validate it with real users

While checking my priorities, I wrote down these questions. All can be answered before writing code.

Question What happens without an answer
Who has this problem now? A wide feature list gets built
How do they solve it today? Alternatives get underestimated
Have they spent money or time on it? Praise gets mistaken for demand
What can be measured a week later? Polish becomes the only signal

Building several candidates and comparing them in internal new game exploration applied part of these questions to games. Faster implementation made several candidates possible. Even so, comparing candidates can’t replace player response.

Here’s how I’d write hypotheses. For Career Center: “People with a scheduled interview will re-answer the same question after their first answer,” measured by the rate of pressing re-answer after the first interview. For PC Supporter: “People about to build their first PC will look up specs themselves when they see a needs-checking mark.” For KBO Fans: “Fans of a team will follow games on the widget without opening the app.” None of these have been tested. Adding measurement to each product isn’t hard, but I’ve done little of gathering users. I expect that to be the first wall in the next stage. As a solo project, sales and support must also come out of my own time. Starting small means reducing the number of products. I haven’t decided which to pick, and the criterion is whether I can meet users now. I may be tempted to pick the one with the most features, but that isn’t market evidence.

Reflection

The counterargument to my judgment is clear. When implementation costs fall, competitors’ costs fall too. Then deep technology may instead become the differentiator. My server-and-client experience in slot work being used to design verification tools supports that side. So I don’t say I’m leaving technology behind.

Another limit is that this post rests only on my experience. It’s my interpretation that AI taking over implementation made the market the bottleneck. I’m writing before putting anything on the market, so it may be wrong.

My sense that AI personas are risky fits here. Career Center’s interviews are fast and specific but don’t show payment or return visits. The Mom Test also covers how to ask about real behavior, not kind words. Virtual interviews are useful for sharpening questions but shouldn’t count as market validation.

Paul Graham stresses that early products go find users directly. It’s what I’ve done least, and tools can’t shrink it.

I also have to admit I’m more comfortable talking about technology. A benchmark or verification tool is a problem I can solve alone. User interviews can end in rejection, and answers aren’t clean. There’s a risk of speaking about the market’s importance while leaning toward more tool work. I’ll keep checking this in later posts.

I’m also wary of studying the market only through books and posts. The references here offer ways of thinking but don’t say what my product’s users want. So this post’s conclusion should be an interview on the calendar, not knowledge. That date doesn’t exist yet.

General lessons

The easier implementation gets, the more I should decide first what to check before starting: one hypothesis, one group of users, one measurement. The Lean Startup’s build-measure-learn loop doesn’t turn when the measure step is empty.

Next steps

I will keep building technical depth as a game developer, but I want to become someone who efficiently makes products that work in the market. None of my personal products has been proven at scale yet. So my next goal is not to build more, but to put one on the market, watch the response, and keep improving it.

The concrete next task is picking one product and scheduling real user interviews. I’ll ask who is solving this problem now and how, before what to build. I’ll write up the results, good or bad, in a later post.
